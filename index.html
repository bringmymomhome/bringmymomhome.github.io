Who she is

Her story

My mother, Alevtina Soboleva, is 59 years old. She came to the United States lawfully in October 2021 and applied for political asylum in April 2022. That application has been pending ever since — and she has been fully compliant at every step.

She built a quiet, careful life in Sunnyvale, California. She holds a valid work permit, a California REAL ID, and active medical insurance. Since 2023 she has been an ESL student at Mission College in Santa Clara, learning English one class at a time. She has no criminal record of any kind — no arrests, no charges, no convictions.

She lives with me — her daughter, Daria, a lawful permanent resident and Head Research Scientist at Cerebras Systems. Our home is her home, and I am prepared to take custody of her the moment she is released.

On July 20, she was stopped at the Buffalo airport before her flight home by three plain-clothes officers who called it a document check. No documents were requested. She has not been home since.

What happened

9 days. Three facilities. No court record.

  1. July 20 Buffalo, New York

    Detained at the airport before her flight home by three plain-clothes officers who called it a document check. No documents were requested. The family was told she would stay in Buffalo a couple of days and could then transfer to California — that there would be no handcuffs, and that mother and daughter would be able to talk.

  2. July 21 Mahoning County Jail, Ohio

    Moved overnight. The family learned of the transfer only from ICE's online locator. Staff took the family's number and promised a call in a few hours. None was permitted.

  3. July 22 Basile, Louisiana

    Moved again, by air, to the South Louisiana ICE Processing Center — her third facility in under three days. Her attorney entered an appearance (Form G-28).

  4. July 23 Staged for a removal flight

    She waited half a day at an airport before being returned to Basile. In a written email, a facility official told her attorney she was deported — while ICE's own locator showed her in custody at Basile. The office of Rep. Ro Khanna (CA-17) engages.

  5. July 24 First phone call

    Four days after her detention, her first call with her daughter. Congressional staff confirm in writing that she appears on ICE's locator while no record of her case exists in the immigration court's public system.

  6. July 25 First video contact

    She is visibly ill — a fever with no medication, no warm clothing against the facility's cold, and no glasses, which she needs to read forms or use the phone system.

  7. July 26 Locked out of court

    Her bond motion is ready but cannot be filed: six days after her detention, her case still does not appear in the immigration court system. She has had no confidential call with her attorney.

  8. The last stop Sunnyvale, California — home

    Not yet.

Current concerns

What she is facing right now

Her firsthand account

In her calls of July 24–25, she described being held for hours in vehicles and aircraft with restroom access and drinking water refused, and being unable to eat for her first three days in custody.

Medical neglect

She is sick with a fever and a severe sore throat and is receiving no medication. Warm clothing and painkillers given to her by other detainees were confiscated by staff. She is without her glasses, which she needs to read forms or use the phone system.

No attorney access

Six days into her detention, she has had no confidential consultation with her legal counsel.

Locked out of court

Her case does not appear in the immigration court system, so a bond request cannot be filed — while the risk of another no-notice transfer, and of deportation to Russia, the very danger underlying her asylum claim, remains.

The family asks ICE to

Five things — none of them extraordinary

  1. Provide immediate medical evaluation and treatment, and basic necessities: medication, warm clothing, and her glasses.

  2. Ensure uninterrupted, confidential access between Ms. Soboleva and her legal counsel.

  3. Enter her case into the immigration court system so her bond request can be heard — without further transfers.

  4. Transfer her to California, where her attorney, family, and support network are located.

  5. Exercise prosecutorial discretion to release her into her daughter's custody while her asylum case proceeds.
