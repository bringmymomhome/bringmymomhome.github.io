Bring her home.

Alevtina Soboleva, 59 — an asylum seeker from Sunnyvale, California, with no criminal record — was detained at the Buffalo airport on July 20, boarding her flight home to California, and moved through three facilities in under three days. She lives with a progressive neurological disorder and is ill with an untreated fever; her first call with her attorney came only in her second week — on the facility's recorded line, never yet a confidential one. For nine days her case appeared nowhere in the immigration court system. Her first hearing before a judge is set for August 12 — day 24 of her detention.

Who she is

Her story

My mother, Alevtina Soboleva, is 59 years old. She came to the United States lawfully in October 2021 and applied for political asylum in April 2022. That application has been pending ever since — and she has been fully compliant at every step.

She built a quiet, careful life in Sunnyvale, California. She holds a valid work permit, a California REAL ID, and active medical insurance. Since 2023 she has been an ESL student at Mission College in Santa Clara, learning English one class at a time. She has no criminal record of any kind — no arrests, no charges, no convictions.

She lives with me — her daughter, Daria, a lawful permanent resident and Head Research Scientist at Cerebras Systems. Our home is her home, and I am prepared to take custody of her the moment she is released.

On July 20 — at the end of a short trip to see Niagara Falls, a childhood dream of hers, on the U.S. side — she was stopped at the Buffalo airport before her flight home to California by three plain-clothes officers who called it a document check. No documents were requested. She has not been home since. She has never left the United States in the nearly five years since she arrived.

What happened

9 days. Three facilities. A first hearing on day 24.

  1. July 20 Buffalo, New York

    Returning to California from a short domestic trip to Niagara Falls, she is detained at the airport before her flight home by three plain-clothes officers who called it a document check. No documents were requested. The family was told she would stay in Buffalo a couple of days and could then transfer to California — that there would be no handcuffs, and that mother and daughter would be able to talk.

  2. July 21 Mahoning County Jail, Ohio

    Moved overnight. The family learned of the transfer only from ICE's online locator. Staff took the family's number and promised a call in a few hours. None was permitted.

  3. July 22 Basile, Louisiana

    Moved again, by air, to the South Louisiana ICE Processing Center — her third facility in under three days. Her attorney entered an appearance (Form G-28).

  4. July 23 Staged for a removal flight

    She waited half a day at an airport before being returned to Basile. In a written email, a facility official told her attorney she was deported — while ICE's own locator showed her in custody at Basile. The office of Rep. Ro Khanna (CA-17) engages.

  5. July 24 First phone call

    Four days after her detention, her first call with her daughter. Congressional staff confirm in writing that she appears on ICE's locator while no record of her case exists in the immigration court's public system.

  6. July 25 First video contact

    She is visibly ill — a fever with no medication, no warm clothing against the facility's heavy industrial air conditioning, and no glasses, which she needs to read forms or use the phone system.

  7. July 26 Locked out of court

    Her bond motion is ready but cannot be filed: six days after her detention, her case still does not appear in the immigration court system. She has had no confidential call with her attorney.

  8. July 29 On the docket at last

    Nine days after her detention, her case is entered into the immigration court system — the first day her attorney could ask a judge for anything on her behalf. For those nine days she could be held, moved, and staged for removal, but could not request release.

  9. July 30 First call with her attorney

    Ten days after the airport, her first phone conversation with her legal counsel since her detention — placed on the facility's standard recorded tablet line, not a confidential legal call.

  10. August 12 · Scheduled Her first day in court — two hearings

    On August 12 — day 24 of her detention — she is scheduled to stand before an immigration judge for the first time. Two hearings are set for that day: a master calendar hearing, where the government's charges against her will be read, and a bond hearing, where the judge can consider her release.

  11. What her family is fighting for Home with her family — Sunnyvale, California

    Not yet.

Her health

Her health, plainly stated

Her family did not plan to make her medical history public. On day 11 of her detention, they no longer feel they can keep it private. What follows is stated as plainly as possible — every condition below predates her detention and is documented in her medical records in California.

Cerebellar ataxia
A progressive neurological disorder affecting balance and coordination — diagnosed before her detention. Her doctors had projected that she could lose the ability to walk, and be in a wheelchair, within a few years; that she was still walking at all, they considered remarkable. In her calls from detention she reports that she now feels too unsteady to cross a room without keeping a hand on the wall.
Stroke risk factors
High cholesterol and prediabetes — elevated blood sugar — both diagnosed. During the July 22–23 transfers — restrained for hours in a small enclosure, refused water — she feared she was having a stroke.
Hearing loss, left ear
She does not hear in her left ear and uses a prescribed hearing aid. It is not with her in detention. Without it — and without her glasses — the facility's phone and tablet systems are close to unusable for her, which is part of why reaching her own family took days.
Untreated illness
A fever, severe sore throat, and headaches, reported in every call since July 24. She reports she has been given no medication, and that warm clothing and medicine shared with her by other detainees were confiscated by staff.

Taken together: she is 59, with a progressive balance disorder that now keeps one hand on the wall when she walks, an untreated fever, hearing she cannot correct without her hearing aid, sight she cannot correct without her glasses, and two diagnosed stroke risk factors — compounded by menopause — in a facility cold enough that detainees wear layers she does not have. Her family is not asking anyone to take their word for any of this. The records exist.

Sources: her medical records in California; her calls of July 24–30, as relayed by her family.

Current concerns

What she is facing right now

Her firsthand account

In her calls of July 24–25, she described being held for hours in vehicles and aircraft with restroom access and drinking water refused, and being unable to eat for her first three days in custody.

Her health

She is ill and untreated — a fever and severe sore throat, with no medication — while living with a progressive neurological disorder, hearing loss, and two diagnosed stroke risk factors. The full medical picture is above.

No confidential attorney access

Her first call with her legal counsel came only in her second week of custody — on the facility's recorded tablet system, the same line used for every other call. As of July 30 she has had no confidential attorney-client consultation. Her attorney is in California, roughly 2,000 miles from the Louisiana facility where she is held; her initial hearing is August 12.

Locked out of court

For nine days, her case appeared nowhere in the immigration court system, making a bond request impossible to file. It has now been entered, with a first hearing set for August 12 — while the risk of another no-notice transfer, and of deportation to Russia, the very danger underlying her asylum claim, remains.

Why return is not an option

Deportation would send a 59-year-old woman, alone, back to a country at war — one where criticizing that war is now a crime, and where having sought political asylum in the United States is itself viewed by the authorities with hostility. Everyone she has — her daughter, her family, her community, everyone who could care for her — is in California. The specific basis of her asylum claim is before USCIS and is deliberately not detailed on this page, to protect her case and the people connected to it.

The family asks ICE to

Five things — none of them extraordinary

  1. Provide immediate medical evaluation and treatment — including for her progressive neurological disorder — and her basic medical equipment: medication, warm clothing, her glasses, and her hearing aid.

  2. Ensure uninterrupted, confidential access between Ms. Soboleva and her legal counsel.

  3. Hear her bond request promptly, and keep her where she is — no further no-notice transfers before her August 12 hearing.

  4. Transfer her to California, where her attorney, family, and support network are located.

  5. Exercise prosecutorial discretion to release her into her daughter's custody while her asylum case proceeds.
